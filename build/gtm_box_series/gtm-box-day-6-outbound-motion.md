Cold outreach is not dead. Lazy outreach is. An outbound motion that books meetings starts with a reason to write today, not a bigger list and a louder template.

## Why timing beats volume

Yesterday you signed a one page plan that gave outbound a share of pipeline and an owner. Today that owner gets a machine to run.

Look at how the work has moved. On my [B2B playbook](https://syedcreates.com/playbook) page, the old model puts 30% of effort into sending cold emails. The 2026 model puts 15% into signal based warm outbound. Half the effort, aimed better. The difference is a trigger. Something changed at the account, and your message is about that change.

Fivos Aresti, cofounder of Workflows.io, laid out a concrete version in [an outbound playbook for Kyle Poyar's Growth Unhinged](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/an-outbound-playbook-for-2025). His team pulled about 66,000 companies from five data sources, filtered and narrowed them to 5,700 VC funded HubSpot users, and scored those against their ICP. Accounts were tiered. A Dream 150 got strictly manual LinkedIn prospecting. Tier 1 got calls and semi automated sequences. Lower tiers got automated email and LinkedIn. On top of that steady layer, they ran campaigns off 13 signals, from website visitors to champion job changes to closed lost deals reopening. Aresti writes that Workflows.io hit $1M ARR three months after launch.

The detail I like most is his case for outbound in the first place. With content led lead generation, "you can't choose who's going to book a meeting with you." Outbound is how you choose. The cold layer keeps your best fit accounts covered every week, and the signals tell you which of them to hit first.

So the motion has five parts.

1. A list built from your ICP, split into tiers.
2. Triggers that give each message a reason to exist.
3. A short multichannel sequence, six touches over two weeks.
4. A talk track and objection answers for the moments a human picks up.
5. A daily cadence with numbers you check every evening.

Phase two of [GTM in a box](https://syedcreates.com/gtm-in-a-box) builds exactly this: lists, sequences and talk tracks, handed to your team to run. The rest of this issue gives you each part, the sequence template and the call script.

## Build the list from your ICP

Start from the segments on your one page plan, not from a database filter you liked.

1. Pull every account that matches segment A and B. Remove anything that hits a disqualifier.
2. Tier them. Tier 1 is your top 50, researched by hand and called. Tier 2 is the next 200, semi automated with a personal first line. Tier 3 is everyone else, automated email only.
3. Pick two or three contacts per account. The person who feels the pain, the person who owns the budget and a likely champion.
4. Research Tier 1 properly. The free [company research tool](https://syedcreates.com/company-research) gives you customers, competitors and pain points from a homepage in a minute.
5. Point the list at the same accounts as your content and partner plays. In the [GTM case study](https://syedcreates.com/gtm-case-study), outbound and partner plays aimed at the same accounts as the content, so each channel warmed up the others.

## Pick triggers that give you a reason to write

A trigger is only useful if it turns into a "why now" sentence. Use one per message.

| Trigger | Where you find it | The why now line |
|---|---|---|
| New security or engineering leader | LinkedIn job changes | New leaders usually review the stack in their first 90 days |
| Job post naming your category | Careers page, job boards | You are hiring for this problem, here is how peers cover it without the hire |
| Funding round | Press, Crunchbase | New money usually means new audit and scale pressure |
| Audit or compliance deadline | Regulator calendars, their own posts | The deadline is fixed, the evidence work is not |
| Visit to pricing or comparison page | Website visitor tool | You were comparing options, here is the short version |
| Champion moved companies | CRM plus LinkedIn | You used us before, the same problem probably exists here |
| Closed lost six months ago | CRM | What changed since we last spoke |

When an account shows two or more triggers, move it up a tier for the month.

## The 6 touch multichannel sequence

Six touches across 14 days. Every email stays under 90 words, plain text, one ask.

| Touch | Day | Channel | Message skeleton |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | Email | Trigger in one line. The problem it usually creates. One peer style result. Ask for 20 minutes. |
| 2 | 2 | LinkedIn | Connection request with a short note about the trigger. No pitch. |
| 3 | 4 | Phone | Permission based opener, talk track below. Voicemail of 20 seconds that points to your email. |
| 4 | 7 | Email | New angle. One proof point or a short resource that answers a question they likely have. Same ask. |
| 5 | 10 | LinkedIn | Comment on a recent post or send a two line message with the resource. |
| 6 | 14 | Email and phone | Direct ask with two specific time slots. Call the same day. |

No breakup email. Touch six offers times, because the goal is a meeting, not a polite goodbye. If you need a sharper core message for touch one, run it through the [one line positioning test](https://syedcreates.com/one-line-positioning-test) first.

## A call talk track that earns 30 seconds

Gong Labs studied more than 300 million cold calls, written up by Armand Farrokh in [the best and worst cold call openers](https://www.gong.io/resources/labs/the-best-and-worst-cold-call-openers-backed-by-data-from-300m-calls/). "Heard the name tossed around?" had an 11.24% success rate, and the permission based opener was close behind at 11.18%. "Did I catch you at a bad time?" came in at 2.15%. The advice under the numbers is simple: "The most important part is to lead with context."

Here is a permission based track built on that.

1. Context. "Hi Priya, this is Sam from [company]. I saw you took over platform security last month."
2. Permission. "This is a cold call. Can I take 30 seconds to say why I called, and you tell me if it is worth a chat?"
3. Problem. "Teams like yours in fintech usually find runtime alerts piling up right before the audit. Peers fixed it by [one line result]."
4. Question. "How are you handling that today?"
5. Ask. "Worth 20 minutes on Tuesday or Thursday to compare notes?"

## Objection handling

| Objection | What it usually means | Response skeleton |
|---|---|---|
| Send me an email | Get off the phone | "Happy to. What should it cover so it is worth opening?" |
| We already use a vendor | Change feels risky | "Most teams we talk to do. Where does it fall short today?" |
| No budget | Not a priority yet | "Fair. When does planning start, and who owns that line?" |
| Not right now | Bad timing or no pain | "Understood. What would need to change for this to matter?" |
| I am not the right person | Wrong contact, or a brush off | "Thanks. Who owns this, and can I mention you sent me?" |

## Daily cadence and weekly numbers

Outbound rewards routine. One rep's day looks like this.

- First hour. Trigger scan and research for Tier 1.
- Next 90 minutes. Calls, while the research is fresh.
- 30 minutes. LinkedIn touches.
- Afternoon. Email batch, then replies.
- Last 15 minutes. Log every outcome in the CRM.

Track six numbers weekly. Accounts entered into sequence, contacts touched, connect rate on calls, positive reply rate, meetings booked and meetings held. Report meetings held and opportunities created in the weekly pipeline review from your one page plan.

## Worked example with illustrative numbers

All numbers below are made up to show the shape. One rep adds 150 accounts a month with two contacts each, so 300 people.

- Email. A 4% positive reply rate gives 12 meeting requests.
- Phone. Calling 150 Tier 1 and Tier 2 contacts at a 10% connect rate gives 15 conversations. Booking a third gives 5 meetings.
- LinkedIn. Adds 3 meetings.
- Total. 20 meetings booked, 16 held at an 80% show rate, and 6 qualified opportunities at about 40%.
- Pipeline. At a $30K average deal, that is $180K of new pipeline per rep per month.

## Common mistakes

- Building the list before the ICP. You end up writing to people who can never buy.
- Using one trigger for the whole list. A funding round is not a reason to email the intern.
- Automating Tier 1. Your best 50 accounts deserve a human.
- Measuring sends. Count meetings held, not emails out.
- Ending with a breakup email. Close with time slots instead.

## What to do today

1. Tier your accounts and pick your top 50.
2. Choose three triggers from the table and write one why now line for each.
3. Write touch one and touch four for your top segment.
4. Practise the talk track out loud five times, then make five calls.
5. Add the six weekly numbers to your pipeline review.
