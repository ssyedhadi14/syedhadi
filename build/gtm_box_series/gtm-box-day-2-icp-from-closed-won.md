Ask five people at a startup who the ideal customer is and you get five answers. The founder names the logo they want, sales names the deal that closed last week, marketing names whoever downloaded the most ebooks. Your CRM has a sixth answer, and it is the only one backed by money.

## Why opinions make a bad ICP

Most ICP documents get written in a workshop. Someone puts "mid market tech companies with a security team" on a slide, everyone nods, and the outbound team spends the next quarter emailing thousands of accounts that look right on paper and never buy.

The problem is not effort. The problem is the input. Opinions overweight memorable deals, big logos and recent pain. They underweight the quiet accounts that closed in 40 days, paid full price and renewed without drama.

April Dunford puts the target customer at the end of her positioning process, not the start. In her [quickstart guide to positioning on Lenny's Newsletter](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/positioning), she works from competitive alternatives to differentiated features to value, and only then asks which customers care most about that value. She describes best fit customers as the ones who "really care a lot about your unique value." That is a behaviour, not a demographic. And behaviour shows up in your deal data.

Kyle Poyar's write up of [how Mutiny rebuilt its ICP](https://www.growthunhinged.com/p/icp-marketing-done-right) shows what that looks like in practice. The team scored firmographic, technographic and intent signals, then tested them against closed won accounts as good fit and closed lost accounts as bad fit. The resulting tiers were not close. Tier A accounts converted from lead to opportunity at 30 percent, Tier B at 8 percent, and the lower tiers barely registered. They now revisit the model every six months to a year based on who is closing, renewing and growing.

That is the whole idea of Day 2. Your ICP is a finding, not a decision. You dig it out of won and lost deals, you score it, and you write it down in a format sales and partners can use on a Monday morning.

## The method, step by step

Block three hours. You need CRM access, a spreadsheet and one person from sales who remembers the deals.

### Step 1, pull the deals

Export every closed won and closed lost deal from the last 12 to 24 months. If you have fewer than 30 won deals, go back further or add late stage open deals as a separate group. Keep these columns at minimum.

- Account name, industry, employee count, region
- Deal source (inbound, outbound, partner, referral, event)
- Created date and closed date, so you can compute cycle length
- Contract value (ACV) and any discount given
- Primary buyer title and champion title
- Use case or product line bought
- Loss reason for lost deals
- Renewal or expansion status for won deals

### Step 2, add the trigger

Your CRM rarely records why a deal started. Go through the won deals with your sales person and add one column, the trigger. Examples are a new compliance deadline, a failed audit, a new CISO, a cloud migration or a funding round. Triggers tell outbound when to reach out, which firmographics alone never will.

### Step 3, find the patterns

Group deals into candidate segments. A segment is a combination of industry, size band and use case, for example fintech, 200 to 1,000 employees, compliance automation. For each segment, calculate five numbers.

1. Win rate, won deals divided by all closed deals
2. Median ACV
3. Median cycle length in days
4. Discount rate, the average discount on won deals
5. Retention signal, the share of won deals that renewed or expanded

Then note the most common buyer title, champion title and trigger in each group.

### Step 4, score the segments

Score each segment from 1 to 5 on each number, then weight it. Use the scorecard below. The top one or two segments become your ICP. The bottom segments become your disqualifiers, which matter just as much.

### Step 5, write the ICP and persona sheet

Fill in the template below. Keep it to one page per segment. Share it with sales, marketing and your partners, and ask each team to flag the last three deals that contradict it.

### ICP and persona sheet

Copy this block once per segment.

- Segment name: ______
- Industry and sub vertical: ______
- Company size band (employees or revenue): ______
- Regions: ______
- Tech stack signals: ______
- Top three triggers: ______, ______, ______
- Main use case: ______
- Competitive alternative they use today: ______
- Median ACV and cycle length: ______ and ______ days
- Economic buyer title: ______
- Champion title: ______

- Evidence, deals this is based on: ______

The last line is the one people skip. Do not skip it. An ICP that cannot point to real deals is an opinion again.

## Worked example

The numbers below are illustrative, made up to show the method, not from any real company.

A cloud security startup exports 60 closed deals, 24 won and 36 lost. Three segments stand out.

| Segment (illustrative) | Win rate | Median ACV | Median cycle | Discount | Renewed |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fintech, 200 to 1,000 staff, compliance | 55% | $48K | 62 days | 8% | 90% |
| Healthcare SaaS, 50 to 200 staff, audit prep | 40% | $22K | 45 days | 15% | 75% |
| Enterprise manufacturing, 5,000+ staff | 15% | $120K | 210 days | 30% | 50% |

Scored on the weights above (illustrative scores in brackets for win rate, ACV, cycle, renewal and discount), fintech lands at about 4.6 out of 5 (5, 4, 4, 5, 5), healthcare SaaS at about 3.6 (4, 2, 5, 4, 3) and enterprise manufacturing at about 2.2 (1, 5, 1, 2, 1). The manufacturing deals felt like the best ones because the logos were big. The data says they took three times longer, needed heavy discounts and renewed half the time.

So the ICP becomes fintech compliance first and healthcare SaaS second, with a clear disqualifier. Walk away from enterprise manufacturing without an active audit trigger. The most common trigger in the fintech wins was a regulator deadline, so outbound now watches for regulatory announcements instead of headcount growth.

If you want to test a target account against the sheet quickly, run its homepage through the [company research tool](https://syedcreates.com/company-research) and check the result against your disqualifiers.

## Common mistakes

- Using only won deals. Lost deals show you who looks like a fit and is not. Without them you cannot write disqualifiers.
- Averaging everything. One giant deal skews a mean. Use medians.
- Confusing a big logo with a good customer. Check cycle length, discount and renewal before you celebrate.
- Leaving out the trigger. Firmographics tell you who. Triggers tell you when, and timing wins outbound.
- Writing it once. Revisit the ICP every quarter or after a major product change. Mutiny does it every six months to a year for a reason.
- Keeping it inside marketing. If sales and partners do not use the sheet, it does not exist. In the [GTM case study](https://syedcreates.com/gtm-case-study), the ICP turned into target account lists for sales, and outbound and partner plays pointed at the same accounts. That shared list is what made it real.

## What to do today

1. Export your closed won and closed lost deals from the last 12 to 24 months.
2. Sit with one sales person for an hour and add the trigger to every won deal.
3. Group deals into three to five segments and fill in the scorecard.
4. Write the ICP and persona sheet for your top segment, with the evidence line filled in.
5. Send it to sales and partners and ask for three deals that contradict it.
