You do not need an analyst team to know who you are up against. You need the buyer's shortlist, five open browser tabs and the win loss notes you collected yesterday. By tonight you will have a grid, a pricing teardown and one battlecard your sellers can use on their next call.

## The competitor you keep forgetting

Ask a founder to name the competition and you get a list of logos from an analyst slide. Ask a buyer and you often get a different answer. "We were going to keep doing it with spreadsheets." "Our platform team was going to script it." "We decided to wait a year."

April Dunford calls these the competitive alternatives, meaning what the customer would do if your product did not exist. In her post on [positioning and competition](https://aprildunford.substack.com/p/positioning-and-competition) she notes that enterprise software teams typically lose 20% to 30% of deals to "no decision." The status quo is cheap, already running and usually good enough. If your battlecards only cover other vendors, you are unarmed in roughly one deal out of four.

She also warns about phantom competitors, vendors your team obsesses over that rarely appear in real deals. Positioning against them dilutes your message. Watch them, but do not build your pitch around them.

Pricing has the same blind spot. Wynter asked 50 senior B2B SaaS leaders how they set prices and found that [62% gate pricing behind "contact sales"](https://wynter.com/post/b2b-saas-pricing-data), and 58% have never tested their own pricing page. The top wish on their list was better competitor pricing data, at 24%. In other words, most of your rivals are guessing too. A disciplined two hour teardown already puts you ahead.

In product marketing at AccuKnox, competitive work ran on a repeatable cadence rather than as a once a year deck. Analyst reports became narrative briefs, "vs X and alternatives" pages fed inbound, and recurring objections turned into battlecards. You can see that list of eleven inbound engines on the [GTM engine](https://syedcreates.com/gtm-engine) page. The point is not volume. The point is that competitive intel only matters when it shows up in a live deal.

This scan is item three of phase one in [GTM in a box](https://syedcreates.com/gtm-in-a-box). Below the line you get the five step method, the grid, a battlecard template and a worked example.

## The five step scan

![Five step competitor scan, pick real alternatives, build the comparison grid, tear down pricing pages, mine reviews and win loss notes, ship the battlecard](/img/blog/gtm-box-day-4-competitor-pricing-scan-1.png)

### Step 1, pick the real alternatives (20 minutes)

Open yesterday's win loss sheet and look at the "competitor named by buyer" column. Add the CRM competitor field and ask two sellers which names come up on calls. Then cut the list to five at most.

- The status quo, whatever the buyer does today
- An internal build, if technical buyers mention it
- The two or three vendors that appear in real deals

Anything else goes on a watch list. Revisit it each quarter, and promote a name only when it shows up in actual deals.

### Step 2, build the comparison grid (40 minutes)

Rows are buying criteria, not your feature list. Pull them from your win loss codes and buyer quotes. Columns are you plus each alternative. Score each cell from 1 to 3 and, this is the rule that matters, every score needs a source link. A public product page, a doc page, a review or a buyer quote. No source, no score.

### Step 3, tear down the pricing pages (20 minutes per alternative)

Capture the same fields for every alternative.

- Pricing model, such as per seat, per usage unit or platform fee
- Value metric, the thing the price scales with
- Entry price, or "contact sales" if hidden
- Tiers, and what each one is clearly built for
- What sits behind the enterprise tier, such as SSO, audit logs or support
- Free trial, free tier or paid pilot
- Annual discount or minimum contract signals

The Internet Archive's Wayback Machine shows older versions of most pricing pages. If a rival changed its tiers recently, that is a signal about where it wants to grow. Write down what you see, not what you assume.

### Step 4, mine reviews and win loss notes (45 minutes)

Read review sites such as G2, Gartner Peer Insights and Capterra, plus the communities your buyers use. Skip the five star and one star extremes and read the three and four star reviews, where buyers explain tradeoffs. Tag each review with one like and one dislike, then merge those tags with your own buyer quotes from yesterday. Patterns that show up in both places are the ones you can trust.

### Step 5, turn it into battlecards (30 minutes each)

One page per alternative, written for a seller in the middle of a call. Start with the status quo, because it is in almost every deal.

## Rules for fair competitive content

- Never trash a competitor. Buyers notice, and it makes you look small.
- Only state what you can link to a public source or a buyer told you directly.
- Date stamp every card and name an owner.
- Refresh quarterly, or sooner when a rival changes pricing or ships something big.

## Worked example

These details are illustrative, invented to show the format.

A seed stage developer tool startup reviews 20 recent decisions. Buyers named Vendor A in six deals, Vendor B in three, and in seven deals chose to keep their homegrown scripts. Four deals named nobody. The vendor the founders worried about most appeared zero times, so it moves to the watch list.

The grid scores time to first value from buyer quotes. Vendor A scores 2, Vendor B 1, homegrown scripts 3 because they already run, and the startup 2. That honest 3 for the status quo is the insight. The pitch cannot be "faster setup." It has to be "the scripts break every quarter and nobody owns them."

The pricing teardown shows Vendor A hides prices behind a demo request, while Vendor B lists a per seat price with SSO gated to its top tier. The startup decides to keep SSO in every plan and say so in its pricing table, a fact it can prove.

Here is the first battlecard, against the status quo.

- Alternative: homegrown scripts. Owner: PMM. Updated quarterly.
- What the buyer is really choosing: no new budget, and risk carried by one engineer.
- We win when: the script owner left, an audit is coming, or the team has grown fast.
- We lose when: the scripts are new and the team is small.
- Discovery questions: Who fixes the scripts when they break? How long did the last fix take? What happens if that person leaves?
- Proof: a two week pilot that replaces one script end to end.
- Never say: "your scripts are bad." Say "you have outgrown them."

## Common mistakes

- Building the grid from your feature list instead of buyer criteria.
- Ignoring the status quo because it has no logo.
- Chasing phantom competitors from analyst slides.
- Making claims you cannot link to a source.
- Writing a ten page battlecard nobody opens on a call.

## What to do today

1. List the alternatives buyers named in your last 20 decisions, including do nothing.
2. Fill in the grid for your top three alternatives, with a source in every scored cell.
3. Tear down each pricing page and save a dated copy of your notes.
4. Write the status quo battlecard first and share it with two sellers for feedback.
