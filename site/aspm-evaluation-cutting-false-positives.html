How I’d Evaluate an ASPM Platform If My Real Goal Was Cutting False Positives

The seven things I would score in any ASPM evaluation when the real goal is fewer, better findings and faster remediation, plus the demo questions to ask.

Syed HadiJul 22, 2026 · Application security

If I were buying an ASPM platform today, I would not start with the feature grid vendors want me to compare.

I would start with a harder question:

Will this platform help my team trust fewer, better findings, and move them through remediation faster?

That question matters because most AppSec teams do not have a scanning problem anymore. They have a signal problem. SAST, DAST, SCA, IaC scanning, secrets detection, container scanning: most teams already run multiple tools. The pain hits later, when those tools dump thousands of findings into dashboards and ticket queues that engineering teams stop taking seriously.

I work at AccuKnox, and one thing I have learned from conversations with security leaders is that “more coverage” sounds good right up until your developers are triaging duplicates from five scanners, arguing about exploitability, and reopening the same class of issue every sprint. That is when application security posture management stops sounding like a category label and starts becoming useful.

If my real goal were ASPM platforms reducing false positives, here is exactly how I would evaluate them.

First, I’d redefine the problem correctly

False positives are not just “wrong findings.”

That is too narrow. In real programs, the pain usually comes from four different sources:

  1. Literal false positives. The scanner is simply wrong.
  2. Duplicate findings. Five tools identify the same issue in slightly different language.
  3. Context-free findings. The issue is technically valid, but low-risk in the actual application path.
  4. Unactionable findings. The issue is real, but the team has no idea who owns it, where it lives, or what fix should come first.

This is why I think many buyers evaluate ASPM the wrong way. They compare integrations, dashboard widgets, and reporting templates before asking whether the platform improves trust in findings.

For me, an ASPM platform is useful only if it helps answer three questions fast:

  • Is this finding real?
  • Does it matter in this application’s current context?
  • Can the right team fix it without extra detective work?

If the product cannot improve those three outcomes, it is just another place to collect noise.

My litmus test: does the platform produce one believable story per risk?

This is the first live demo test I would run.

I would give the vendor a realistic environment: maybe one microservice-based application, a couple of repos, one Kubernetes deployment, a CI/CD pipeline, and findings coming from SAST, SCA, secrets scanning, and container scanning.

Then I would ask them to show me a single risk story. Not just a list of alerts. A story.

For example:

“This exposed Java dependency is present in repo A, built into image B, deployed in namespace C, reachable through service D, and owned by team E. It is internet-facing, exploitable under current runtime conditions, and the fix is to upgrade from version X to version Y.”

That is what I want.

If all I get is a normalized findings table with severity filters, I am not impressed. Aggregation is easy. Correlation is hard.

That is why I frame the evaluation around unified context. Modern teams need visibility beyond isolated scanners, especially when they are trying to connect build-time and runtime reality.

The 7 things I would score in any ASPM evaluation checklist

Here is the practical scorecard I would use.

1. Deduplication quality

Every vendor says they reduce noise. I would want to see how.

Ask:

  • How do you merge findings from SAST, DAST, SCA, IaC, API, and secrets tools?
  • Do you deduplicate by CVE only, or also by asset, package, code location, exploit path, and business context?
  • Can the platform collapse multiple scanner outputs into one remediation item?

This matters because teams do not fix “findings.” They fix issues in systems they own.

A weak ASPM product will show 14 alerts for the same root problem. A strong one will show 1 issue with 14 corroborating signals.

That difference sounds cosmetic until you realize it drives ticket volume, SLA tracking, executive reporting, and developer trust.

2. Reachability and exploitability context

This is where serious false positive reduction starts.

If a platform does not evaluate whether vulnerable code is reachable, whether a package is actually invoked, or whether the service is exposed in practice, it is still prioritizing mostly on static severity.

Static severity alone is a trap.

I have seen teams burn cycles on “critical” findings in dead code while a medium-severity issue in a public-facing API sat untouched because it looked less urgent in the scanner output.

So I would ask:

  • Do you support reachability analysis?
  • Do you correlate findings with internet exposure or runtime usage?
  • Can you combine scanner results with runtime signals or deployment metadata?
  • Do you distinguish “present” from “practically exploitable”?

At AccuKnox, we have spent a lot of time thinking about build-to-runtime correlation, because risk gets much clearer when posture is connected to what is actually running in production.

3. Ownership mapping

This is underrated.

You cannot reduce remediation friction if the platform cannot answer: who owns this?

In a modern environment, the same issue may touch:

  • a repo owner,
  • a service owner,
  • a platform team,
  • a cloud team,
  • and an application team.

If ownership is not mapped automatically, the burden shifts back to the security team. Then your ASPM product becomes another manual routing problem.

I would test for:

  • repository-to-service mapping,
  • asset tagging,
  • business application grouping,
  • team ownership assignment,
  • ticket routing logic,
  • and support for environments where ownership is messy rather than perfect.

A good demo should show me how a finding lands with the right team without a security engineer acting like a human API gateway.

4. Remediation guidance that is actually usable

Here is my cynical view: a lot of “AI-powered remediation” still reads like polished filler attached to a scanner alert.

I would evaluate remediation help very practically.

Can the platform tell me:

  • the exact vulnerable package or code path?
  • the safe target version?
  • whether there is a known fix available?
  • whether compensating controls exist?
  • whether a fix will break other dependencies?
  • how similar issues have been resolved before?

The best remediation guidance removes ambiguity. It does not just add explanation.

For example, “High severity deserialization issue detected” is not helpful.

This is helpful: “Package X in service Y is vulnerable in versions below 4.2.1. The vulnerable function is reachable through endpoint /api/export. The owning team is Payments. Upgrade path has no breaking changes based on dependency graph analysis.”

That is the level of specificity I would want before I believe the platform reduces false positive workload in practice.

5. Workflow fit inside developer reality

This is where many evaluations become theater.

A vendor can demo a polished risk dashboard and still fail in a real engineering organization if the workflow ends there.

Security teams do not close findings. Developers do.

So my ASPM evaluation checklist would include questions like:

  • Does it integrate cleanly with Jira, ServiceNow, GitHub, GitLab, Azure DevOps, or whichever systems the team actually uses?
  • Can it suppress duplicates without losing audit history?
  • Can findings be grouped into one fix ticket?
  • Can I push remediation into pull request flows?
  • Can I set policy gates without overwhelming pipelines?
  • Can teams document accepted risk and exceptions cleanly?

If I have to ask engineers to live in yet another security console, adoption will drop fast.

One thing I tell buyers is simple: tool quality is only half about detection. The other half is behavioral design. The platform has to fit how work already moves.

6. Tuning and suppression controls

No serious buyer should skip this.

Even the best platforms need tuning. Different organizations have different threat models, coding standards, compensating controls, and tolerance for noise. A product that claims to eliminate false positives without tunability is, in my experience, overselling.

I would ask:

  • Can I suppress findings with expiration dates?
  • Can I suppress globally, by repo, by asset group, or by policy?
  • Is there role-based control over who can suppress what?
  • Is suppression auditable?
  • Can I create environment-specific rules?
  • Can I mark findings as “known but accepted” without losing visibility?

This matters even more in regulated environments, where exceptions need to stay visible to both security and compliance teams.

A mature platform should let you reduce noise without creating blind spots.

7. Reporting that reflects real risk reduction, not just finding counts

This might be my biggest pet peeve.

I have seen security dashboards where success is measured by total findings discovered. That is not success. That is inventory.

What I would want instead:

  • duplicates eliminated,
  • mean time to triage,
  • mean time to remediation,
  • exploitable findings reduced,
  • internet-exposed severe issues reduced,
  • reopened findings rate,
  • team-by-team remediation performance,
  • and trend lines showing posture improvement over time.

If an ASPM tool helps me close 500 meaningless alerts, that is less valuable than helping me close 20 real, high-confidence risks that affect production systems.

The point is not to look busy. The point is to become more credible.

The demo questions I’d ask every vendor

If I were running a buying process tomorrow, I would ask these directly:

  1. Show me how you merge findings from multiple scanners into one remediation object.
  2. Show me how you determine reachability or exploitability.
  3. Show me how a developer knows this issue belongs to their team.
  4. Show me how to suppress noise without hiding real risk.
  5. Show me how this finding becomes a ticket or PR task.
  6. Show me what changes between a raw scanner alert and a prioritized ASPM issue.
  7. Show me metrics proving remediation got faster or more accurate after deployment.

I say “show me” intentionally.

I do not want answerware. I want workflow evidence.

My personal rule: beware platforms that are broad but shallow

This is probably the most opinionated part of my view.

I am cautious of platforms that claim to do everything but cannot go deep on correlation logic. In AppSec, breadth is easy to market because buyers love the idea of consolidation. But if consolidation just creates one giant alert bucket, you have not solved the actual problem.

A platform can ingest SAST, DAST, SCA, secrets, containers, APIs, and IaC findings and still fail the most important test: does the security team trust what rises to the top?

That is why I would rather buy a platform with strong normalization, ownership mapping, and remediation workflows than one with 40 loosely connected modules.

In practice, teams remember one thing after rollout:

Did the platform make life quieter and clearer, or just busier and prettier?

What good looks like in the first 90 days

If an ASPM deployment is working, I would expect to see some concrete outcomes in the first quarter:

  • fewer duplicate tickets reaching engineering,
  • better agreement between AppSec and developers on what is worth fixing first,
  • shorter triage cycles,
  • more confidence in severity,
  • cleaner ownership routing,
  • better reporting to leadership on actual risk reduction.

I would not expect magic. False positives never fully disappear. But I would expect a noticeable improvement in the ratio of noise to action.

That ratio matters more than almost any product claim.

Final thought: I wouldn’t buy an ASPM platform to “see more”

I would buy it to argue less.

Less argument about whether a finding is real.

Less argument about whether it matters.

Less argument about who owns it.

Less argument about what to fix first.

That, to me, is the real value of application security posture management.

So if I were evaluating vendors today, my scorecard would stay simple:

Does this platform help my team trust fewer, better findings?

Because once trust goes up, remediation speed usually follows. When remediation speed rises, security starts to feel like part of engineering momentum instead of a tax on it.

That is the standard I would use. Honestly, it is the only one I think matters.
